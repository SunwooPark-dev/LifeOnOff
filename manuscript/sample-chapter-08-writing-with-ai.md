# Sample Chapter — Writing with AI

## Chapter 8. Writing with AI

A few years ago, bad writing was usually slow.

It took time to produce clumsy memos, bloated emails, vague strategy drafts, and lifeless thought pieces. Someone had to sit there and type every unnecessary word. Today, bad writing can arrive at high speed. It can be grammatically clean, neatly formatted, plausible in tone, and completely forgettable. AI has made it easier than ever to produce language that looks finished before it has earned the right to exist.

Writing makes both sides visible at once: the promise of faster thinking and the risk of easier emptiness.

Used well, AI can help a writer think faster, explore more options, improve structure, reduce friction, and revise with more stamina than most people can manage alone. Used badly, it can turn a living idea into generic paste. It can sand away tension, flatten voice, and create a kind of professional fog: language that sounds competent enough to survive but not strong enough to matter.

The question is not whether AI can help you write.

It can.

The question is whether you will use it to increase the quality of your thinking or merely to increase the speed of your output.

In practice, that means defining the writing job clearly before you delegate a single sentence.

## The wrong way to write with AI

Most weak AI-assisted writing follows the same sequence.

A person begins with only a vague idea of what they want to say. Instead of clarifying the argument, they push the ambiguity directly into the model. The model responds with a respectable-looking draft. The draft is then lightly edited, reformatted, and sent onward. At every step, the writer feels productive. In reality, the hardest part of writing — deciding what matters, what the argument is, what the tone should be, and what the reader truly needs — was outsourced before it was even understood.

This creates four common problems.

### 1. The draft is fluent but uncommitted
It says many acceptable things and almost nothing exact. Every sentence sounds like it came from a person trying not to be wrong.

### 2. The structure is clean but generic
The writing follows recognizable patterns because the model is good at default patterns. That makes it readable, but not memorable.

### 3. The voice disappears
If the writer does not actively preserve tone, point of view, and standards, the output drifts toward polished sameness.

### 4. The writer stops thinking early
The draft arrives so fast that the writer mistakes generation for progress. They switch from author to editor before the underlying idea becomes strong enough.

These are not technology problems. They are workflow problems.

## The right question: What part of writing are you delegating?

Writing is not one activity. It is several different activities that often get collapsed into one word.

When you say, “I need to write,” you may actually mean one of the following:
- discover what you think,
- find the central argument,
- create a structure,
- produce a first draft,
- sharpen language,
- vary tone for a different audience,
- compress length,
- strengthen clarity,
- improve rhythm,
- fact-check claims,
- or review for weaknesses.

AI performs differently on each of these jobs.

It is usually strong at:
- generating options,
- proposing structures,
- rephrasing,
- summarizing,
- tightening language,
- and helping you compare versions.

It is much weaker at:
- knowing what is truly worth saying,
- deciding what should be omitted,
- understanding what risk you are willing to take,
- maintaining hard-won taste,
- and protecting the emotional center of a piece.

So before using AI, ask a better question:

**Which part of writing am I delegating, and which part must remain mine?**

That one question prevents a large amount of mediocre output.

## A high-agency workflow for writing with AI

Here is a practical workflow that keeps the writer in the right role: let AI help generate and reshape material, but keep the judgment that determines meaning and send-readiness close to you.

### Step 1. Define the writing job before asking for language
Before opening the model, define the writing job in plain language. Then write down the operating details:
1. **Audience** — Who is this for?
2. **Purpose** — What should change after they read it?
3. **Standard** — What does “good” look like here?
4. **Constraint** — What must this draft avoid?

Example:
- Audience: busy executives
- Purpose: approve a project rescope
- Standard: concise, confident, specific
- Constraint: must not sound defensive or overly technical

This takes one minute and often saves thirty.

### Step 2. Ask for structure before prose
If your thinking is still forming, do not ask for a full draft yet. Ask for:
- possible angles,
- competing outlines,
- a recommended structure,
- objections a reader may have,
- or missing questions.

This keeps you in a decision-making posture instead of a passive acceptance posture.

### Step 3. Draft with direction, not with hope
Once you know the shape, use AI to accelerate a constrained task.

Bad prompt:
> Write a professional email about our new strategy.

Better instruction:
> Draft a concise email to our leadership team announcing a rescope of the Q3 launch. The tone should be calm and decisive. Include three reasons for the change, one risk we are reducing, and the next decision needed from leadership. Avoid hype, jargon, and apology language.

The difference is not prompt cleverness. It is editorial clarity.

### Step 4. Review at three levels
Do not stop at “sounds good.” Review in layers.

#### Layer A: Argument
- Is the core point clear?
- Does the piece actually say something?
- Is there a strong center of gravity?

#### Layer B: Structure
- Does the order make sense?
- Is the opening strong enough?
- Are transitions doing real work?

#### Layer C: Language
- Are any phrases generic or inflated?
- Does the tone match the audience?
- Does the writing sound like a person with standards?

AI can help at each of these layers, but you should decide which layer you are working on. Otherwise revision turns into endless surface editing.

### Step 5. Restore specificity
One of the easiest ways to improve AI-assisted writing is to replace abstraction with concrete detail.

Generic sentence:
> Our organization must embrace innovation to remain competitive.

Stronger sentence:
> If we keep routing every product decision through three approval layers, faster competitors will learn from customers before we do.

Models often default to smooth abstraction because abstraction is safer. Good writing usually becomes stronger by getting riskier and more specific.

### Step 6. Protect voice deliberately
If you want the writing to sound like you, you must provide more than topic and tone. You need working editorial rules.

For example:
- prefer short paragraphs,
- avoid corporate euphemisms,
- use one sharp sentence every page,
- never exaggerate certainty,
- keep rhythm varied,
- cut any sentence that sounds borrowed.

Voice is not magic. It is often the residue of repeated choices. AI can preserve those choices only if you name them and enforce them.

Across the whole workflow, keep the argument, tone, and final send visibly yours.

## A real-work scenario

Imagine a strategy lead who needs to write a one-page memo explaining why a team should stop building three features and focus on one.

Without discipline, AI will produce a memo that sounds balanced, professional, and dead. It will mention alignment, priorities, evolving market conditions, and stakeholder value. Nothing will be false. Nothing will be memorable either.

A better workflow would look like this:

1. The writer decides the real message: we are spreading attention too thin and risking a weak launch.
2. The writer identifies the emotional reality: some people will feel like their work is being deprioritized.
3. The writer asks AI for three possible structures:
   - data-first,
   - customer-risk-first,
   - leadership-decision-first.
4. The writer picks one structure and drafts the core paragraph themselves.
5. AI then helps tighten the memo, test alternative openings, and reduce repetition.
6. The writer performs a final pass for courage: does the memo actually say the hard thing clearly?

That is high-agency writing with AI.

The model accelerates. The human decides.

## Where AI helps most in writing

Used correctly, AI is especially valuable for five writing tasks.

### 1. Breaking inertia
AI is excellent at helping you begin when the page is blank and your thinking is partially formed.

### 2. Expanding options
It can quickly generate alternate openings, structures, framings, headlines, and summaries.

### 3. Diagnosing weak spots
It can point out repetition, vagueness, missing transitions, and likely reader objections.

### 4. Adapting for audience
A strong piece may need to be rewritten for executives, customers, internal teams, or the public. AI is very useful for this kind of transformation.

### 5. Tightening and compressing
It can help remove fluff and improve economy, especially when you already know what matters.

## Where writers get into trouble

The biggest mistake is not overusing AI. It is using AI at the wrong stage.

If you use it before you know what you mean, it can blur the thinking.
If you use it after you know exactly what you mean, it can be powerful.

That distinction matters more than almost any prompt technique.

Another mistake is mistaking polished language for finished work. AI often produces copy that is good enough to survive a quick skim. That is dangerous. Many professional environments reward exactly that level of adequacy. But if you want your writing to persuade, lead, or endure, adequacy is not enough.

The final mistake is forgetting that writing is partly a way of thinking. If you outsource all the pressure too early, you may save time while losing the very mental process that would have made the piece worth reading.

## A practical checklist for writing with AI

Before drafting:
- What problem am I solving?
- Do I know the audience?
- Do I know the point?
- Do I know what good looks like?
- Do I know what must not happen?
- Do I know what is at stake if this lands badly?

During drafting:
- Am I asking for structure, language, or both?
- Have I constrained the task clearly?
- Am I still making the key decisions?

During revision:
- Is the argument sharp?
- Is the structure doing its job?
- Is the language alive or generic?
- Does this sound like a person I would trust?

## The deeper point

Writing with AI is not merely about productivity. It reveals what kind of professional you are becoming.

If you use AI to avoid the difficult parts of writing, your prose may become smoother while your thinking becomes weaker. If you use AI to support the difficult parts of writing — to test structure, sharpen expression, surface alternatives, and reduce friction — then the technology can make you more capable rather than less.

That is the standard to aim for.

Do not ask AI to replace your voice.
Ask it to help you hear your own thinking sooner.

Do not ask it to decide what matters.
Ask it to help you build something clearer, stronger, and more exact once you do.

That is how writing stays human, even when the workflow changes.
