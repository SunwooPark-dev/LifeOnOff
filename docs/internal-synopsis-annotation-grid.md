# Internal Synopsis Annotation Grid — The High-Agency AI Worker

## Goal
Merge the per-part annotation outputs into one control grid for drafting, revision, and internal read-through use.

## Status
Completed.

## Usage rules
- Each chapter carries metadata only, not replacement synopsis prose.
- Each chapter keeps exactly three control lines:
  1. must-prove claim
  2. concrete example / case
  3. boundary / non-overlap from neighboring chapters
- Use the grid during revision and read-through as a yes/no control sheet.

## Part I and Part II

### Chapter 1. The Day Work Started Moving Faster Than Judgment
- Must prove: AI changed work tempo before judgment caught up; the core risk is speed-versus-discernment, not automation alone.
- Concrete case: a report / plan / decision / team-guidance cycle where drafts, options, and recommendations arrive before careful evaluation.
- Boundary / non-overlap: do not explain capability taxonomy (Ch 2) or failure-mode diagnosis (Ch 3); stay at problem framing.

### Chapter 2. What AI Is Good At — and What It Will Never Do for You
- Must prove: AI is strong at pattern completion, synthesis, transformation, acceleration, and simulation; judgment, responsibility, value hierarchy, and trust remain human-owned.
- Concrete case: contrast model-assisted drafting / option generation with a human-only value choice or accountability call.
- Boundary / non-overlap: do not slide into misuse diagnosis (Ch 3) or operating-model tactics (Ch 4–7).

### Chapter 3. The Four Failure Modes of AI Work
- Must prove: laziness, generic output, false confidence, and erosion of voice / standards are distinct, recurring failure modes.
- Concrete case: one recognizable work scenario per failure mode, or one composite scenario showing apparently useful AI output degrading quality over time.
- Boundary / non-overlap: keep this diagnostic only; do not prescribe the operating model that belongs in Ch 4–7.

### Chapter 4. Think Before You Prompt
- Must prove: prompting is downstream of work definition; the pre-generation brief must name problem, objective, standard, constraints, and stakes.
- Concrete case: the support-response-time leadership update, with a one-page brief that separates knowns, unknowns, and risk.
- Boundary / non-overlap: do not become a prompt-hacks chapter; defer repeatability to Ch 5, allocation to Ch 6, and quality control to Ch 7.

### Chapter 5. Build Workflows, Not Tricks
- Must prove: durable leverage comes from repeatable workflow architecture, not one-off prompt tricks; workflow needs inputs, transformation, review, decision, and record.
- Concrete case: the Friday leadership brief built from scattered project notes, blockers, decisions, and a provenance trail.
- Boundary / non-overlap: avoid the deeper allocation logic of Ch 6 and the standards / taste system of Ch 7; do not expand into later practical applications (Ch 8–11).

### Chapter 6. Keep the Human in the Loop — but Put the Human in the Right Loop
- Must prove: “human in the loop” requires allocation logic; delegate / supervise / own should follow stakes, reversibility, context load, verification ease, and accountability.
- Concrete case: the support-ops queue split into ticket normalization (delegate), weekly pattern summary (supervise), and escalation boundary decisions (own).
- Boundary / non-overlap: do not restate workflow architecture from Ch 5 or review discipline from Ch 7; keep focus on task allocation and ownership.

### Chapter 7. Taste, Standards, and the Fight Against Generic Output
- Must prove: taste and standards function as review controls that reject plausible-but-generic output and stop drift from becoming the house style.
- Concrete case: an internal brief about a slipping initiative where a polished generic update is replaced by a direct version that preserves uncertainty, tradeoff, and ownership.
- Boundary / non-overlap: do not turn into a writing-only chapter (Ch 8) or revisit the upstream framing / workflow / ownership layers from Ch 4–6.

## Part III and Part IV

### Chapter 8. Writing with AI
- Must prove: AI can speed ideation, structure, drafting, revision, audience adaptation, and compression while the writer keeps meaning, specificity, standards, and final send ownership.
- Concrete case: the strategy memo about cutting three features to one; the reviewer should see the writer draft the core paragraph, then use AI for structure testing, tightening, and alternative openings.
- Boundary / non-overlap: stay on writing stages and editorial control; do not re-argue research verification, planning tradeoffs, or coordination follow-up mechanics.

### Chapter 9. Research with AI
- Must prove: AI is useful for exploration, comparison, and synthesis, but it cannot be treated as a source of truth; verification discipline must dominate before findings are shared.
- Concrete case: the product leader deciding whether to adopt a new AI feature in an enterprise platform; the chapter must show the source ladder, uncertainty tracking, and the split between exploration and verification.
- Boundary / non-overlap: do not repeat Chapter 8’s voice-and-style problem or Chapter 10’s decision-ownership framework; keep the chapter on evidence, source quality, and trust.

### Chapter 10. Planning and Decision-Making with AI
- Must prove: AI widens options and stress-tests assumptions, but the leader owns the decision, the tradeoffs, and the pain accepted by the choice.
- Concrete case: the quarterly launch review where the team narrows scope, builds a tradeoff table, runs a premortem, and writes the recommendation in owned language.
- Boundary / non-overlap: do not drift back into Chapter 9’s verification ladder or Chapter 11’s recap/ownership mechanics; stay on decision preparation and human accountability.

### Chapter 11. Communication, Meetings, and Coordination
- Must prove: AI can improve briefs, summaries, translation, and follow-up drafts, but it cannot create alignment, ownership, or decision traceability by itself.
- Concrete case: the cross-functional launch-scope meeting where AI drafts the recap, then the owner separates decisions, open questions, actions, and interpretations and verifies owners and deadlines.
- Boundary / non-overlap: do not duplicate Chapter 10’s planning table or Chapter 9’s evidence discipline; keep the chapter on communication artifacts, meetings, and handoffs.

### Chapter 12. What Becomes More Valuable When AI Gets Cheap
- Must prove: as competent output gets cheap, judgment, trust, voice, taste, synthesis, and strategic framing become the scarce edge that makes AI-assisted work worth relying on.
- Concrete case: the two managers with the same tools but different outcomes; one only moves faster, while the other deepens the work and produces a stronger recommendation.
- Boundary / non-overlap: do not turn this into Chapter 13’s habit drift or Chapter 14’s closing standard; keep it as the value-shift hinge, not the identity chapter or the rulebook.

### Chapter 13. How to Stay Useful Without Becoming Machine-Shaped
- Must prove: constant AI use can improve usefulness or produce machine-shaped drift; the chapter must show the weekly behavioral drift and the counter-habits that preserve authorship.
- Concrete case: the product ops lead handling onboarding escalations, including prompt relay, pre-thought automation, confidence laundering, and disappearing voice, then the repaired workflow with a non-delegable core.
- Boundary / non-overlap: do not restate Chapter 12’s value hierarchy or Chapter 14’s automate/deepen/refuse memo; stay on weekly behavior, authorship, standards, and self-respect.

### Chapter 14. The High-Agency Future
- Must prove: the final standard is operational — automate the routine, deepen the consequential, refuse the corrosive — so AI use preserves judgment and responsibility.
- Concrete case: the practical memo / next-few-years checklist that maps writing, research, planning, and coordination to automate / deepen / refuse choices.
- Boundary / non-overlap: do not re-run the full chapter-by-chapter argument or drift into Chapter 13’s identity psychology; close by codifying the standard, not by re-explaining the whole book.
